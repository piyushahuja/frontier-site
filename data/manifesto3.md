India's future world leaders have already been born. If we connect them to someone a few steps ahead, early enough, and give them permission to take risks, the reward is this: when a technology's direction is set, we are in the room stewarding it, not waiting to receive it.

Our post-independence science model — the IITs, ISRO, TIFR, the atomic program — was noble and nation-building, but it optimised for broad public uplift and self-sufficiency. This effort is about something narrower and sharper: concentrated excellence that produces a handful of globally dominant enterprises.

Over the past decade, long-term ownership capital came through the border, then plateaued — and barely any of it reached frontier tech. It came, increasingly, to *rent* talent, not to *own* what that talent creates.

That is the gap concentrated excellence closes: not many good companies, but a few of genuine global consequence.

A nation that only imports the frontier cannot help steer it. A country that consumes technology built elsewhere is a client, not a partner. Self-reliance — *atmanirbharta* — was the first step. The frontier company is where self-reliance matures into contribution: to be a friend the world actually needs, India must be a *maker* at the frontier, not merely a market for it.
