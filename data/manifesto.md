%% lead
Every student has a kernel — a life force, a *vita activa* — that holds the potential for their life's best work. Most emerge from the assembly line of family, school, and college with their wings compressed. Some find they have wings and start to spread them, but this can take years. With the right person at the right moment, we can speed that up. And when you speed it up across enough people, something shifts — not just for them, but for the country.

The great poet Mary Oliver asks: *"Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?"* The question lives with an equal sense of anxiety and possibility for every student — and perhaps with a tinge of regret and self-satisfaction for us alumni. We cannot decide for them what they will do with that life. But we know how much it matters to choose the right doors while they are still open.

A nation that only imports the frontier cannot help steer it. A country that consumes technology built elsewhere is a client, not a partner. But nations don't build at the frontier — *people* do, and those people are sitting in lecture halls right now, wings folded, waiting for someone to show them the sky is real.

"We are living through a transformative period in technology, and today's students have an extraordinary opportunity to shape what comes next."
— Soumyadeb Mitra · RudderStack

Our post-independence science model — the IITs, ISRO, TIFR, the atomic program — was noble and nation-building, but it optimised for broad public uplift and self-sufficiency. This effort is about something different: unlocking concentrated excellence, one student at a time, until a handful of them build enterprises of genuine global consequence.

At critical moments, you can raise someone's aspirations simply by suggesting they do something more ambitious than they had in mind. It costs relatively little to do this, but the benefit to them — and to the world — can be enormous. Three things compound in ways most students never hear about early enough:

*Compounding is invisible and decisive.* Learning, capital, and power all compound. Most careers progress linearly; the ones that matter find a compounding curve early.

*Risk is cheaper than you think.* People overestimate risk and underestimate reward. Early in a career there is little to lose and everything to gain. Be willing to let small opportunities go for potential step changes — small bets where you lose 1x if wrong but make 100x if right.

*Own, don't rent.* Most wealth comes not from high salaries but from owning things that increase rapidly in value. Selling your time scales linearly. Owning a venture that grows compounds. Over the past decade, long-term ownership capital came through India's border, then plateaued — and barely any of it reached frontier tech. It came, increasingly, to *rent* Indian talent, not to *own* what that talent creates.

In Joseph Campbell's monomyth, the hero departs the ordinary world when he receives a call to adventure — Frodo in the Shire, Harry Potter at Privet Drive, Luke Skywalker on Tatooine. With the help of a mentor — Gandalf, Hagrid, Obi-Wan — the hero crosses a guarded threshold into a world of wonder. There, assisted by allies, the hero faces an ordeal and rises to it.

We are the mentors and the allies. We are Gandalf, Hagrid. Self-reliance — *atmanirbharta* — was the first step. But for self-reliance to mature into contribution, India must be a *maker* at the frontier, not merely a market for it. That does not happen in policy documents. It happens when one student meets one person a few steps ahead, early enough, and gets permission to take a risk worth taking.
